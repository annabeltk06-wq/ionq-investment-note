IonQ: A Comps-Based View on Quantum Computing

As a materials science undergraduate, I have developed an interest in quantum computing through my exposure to the underlying physical and materials challenges that shape the technology. With a week-long industry experience in the quantum space planned for this September, I wanted to explore the company IonQ not only as is a technically impressive company, but also through the lens of how investors could evaluate its commercial potential. This note is my attempt to bridge that technical interest with a financial perspective, using a comps-based approach rather than a DCF.

I wrote this note a couple of days ago as part of that exploration and wanted to make the work public as a linkable record of my thinking and analysis.

For educational purposes only; not investment advice.

Investment thesis

I see IonQ as one of the more compelling publicly traded ways to gain long-term exposure to quantum computing, particularly because it is building beyond a single quantum-computing product. The company is diversifying into networking, sensing, security and semiconductor manufacturing - all supported by trapped ion quantum computers. My view is that the market may be underpricing the longevity of the quantum-computing industry and IonQ's ability to become a scaled platform if technical milestones translate directly into commercial adoption. 

A near-term catalyst is IonQ's planned Investor Day on September 8, 2026, where I would be looking for greater clarity around the combined IonQ/SkyWater roadmap, commercialization, manufacturing capacity and long term financial targets.

Valuation / comps

For a young industry where current earnings may not be as particularly meaningful, I think EV/revenue is a more useful starting point than P/E, although even revenue multiples should be treated cautiously because quantum revenue is still at an early stage across a lot of companies.

Using approximate late-July 2026 market values and FY2025 revenue:

| Company | Approx. market value | FY2025 revenue | Market Cap / Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **IonQ (IONQ)** | ~$18.9bn | $130.0m | **~145x** |
| **D-Wave (QBTS)** | ~$7.9bn | $24.6m | **~321x** |
| **Rigetti (RGTI)** | ~$6.4bn | $7.1m | **~901x** |
| **Quantum Computing Inc. (QUBT)** | ~$2.0bn range | $0.7m | **>1,000x** |

IonQ's FY2025 revenue was $130.0m, up 202% year over year, while D-Wave reported $24.6m, up 179%, and Rigetti reported $7.1m. QUBT reported only $0.68m of FY2025 revenue, making its multiple difficult to interpret.

I think that this comparison is imperfect because these companies use different quantum architectures and have very different revenue mixes as a result. However, the comparison is potentially useful for showing that IonQ is not necessarily the most expensive quantum stock on a revenue basis despite having the largest commercial revenue base among the key players in the industry. D-Wave's technology also makes it less directly comparable, while Rigetti is primarily pursuing superconducting gate-model systems. QUBT is even less comparable because of its focus on photonics and quantum optics. IonQ's edge is its trapped ion architecture which offers strong qubit connectivity and the potential for highly accurate quantum computations. 

I therefore would not argue that IonQ is "cheap" on conventional valuation metrics. Instead, the argument is that the market is valuing these companies primarily on future quantum optionality, and IonQ's combination of revenue growth, cash resources, technology development and vertical integration could justify a premium within the group of players.

Supporting data points

1. Technical progress and roadmap.
IonQ says it achieved 99.99% two-qubit gate fidelity and has moved toward a semiconductor-based roadmap. The SkyWater transaction is intended to provide domestic foundry access and accelerate IonQ's chip-focused strategy, with the companies targeting very large-scale systems over the next several years.

2. The quantum market may have unusually long-duration optionality.
My personal view is that quantum stocks should be considered on a much longer time horizon than a normal early-stage technology trade. If useful quantum advantage develops across areas such as optimisation, materials, drug discovery, defence and cryptography, today's small revenue bases could eventually represent the early stages of a much larger computing market that is unlike anything that exists now.

Key risks

Technology and commercialization remain the biggest risks. Unfortunately, quantum computing has not yet reached the point where commercial demand can be confidently extrapolated from technical progress. A company can hit impressive qubit or fidelity milestones without those improvements translating into economically valuable workloads.

Tariffs and semiconductor geopolitics are another consideration. Bringing manufacturing onshore could reduce some geopolitical and supply-chain risks, but SkyWater's semiconductor operations still expose the combined company to semiconductor-industry cycles, equipment costs, trade restrictions and changing tariff policy. I would therefore not assume that vertical integration automatically means lower costs.

SkyWater could create both strategic upside and execution risk. I like the logic of owning more of the semiconductor supply chain, particularly given the importance of trusted domestic manufacturing, but IonQ is spending approximately $1bn of cash on the transaction, including roughly $800m of purchase consideration and $200m for debt repayment and other transaction costs. Integration, unexpected capital expenditure, weaker-than-expected SkyWater economics or failure to generate the anticipated synergies could reduce the value of the acquisition.

Conclusion

My view is that the comps support a premium valuation for IonQ, but not an unlimited one. IonQ trades at a substantial multiple of current revenue, yet that premium is arguably more defensible than the valuations of several smaller quantum peers because of its much larger revenue base, rapid growth, significant cash position and broader platform strategy. The September 8 Investor Day is therefore important: I would become more constructive if management demonstrates credible progress on commercialization, technical milestones, SkyWater integration and the long-term revenue opportunity; conversely, I would reconsider the thesis if growth slows materially, quantum milestones fail to translate into customer demand or if SkyWater absorbs substantially more capital than expected.
